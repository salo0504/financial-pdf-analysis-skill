# Document types: what to expect and where to look

## Government budgets & CAFRs / ACFRs (Annual Comprehensive Financial Reports)

Typical structure: transmittal letter → MD&A (Management's Discussion &
Analysis, narrative context and often the best summary of what changed
and why) → basic financial statements (government-wide, then fund-level)
→ notes to the financial statements (critical — this is where accounting
policy, debt, pension, and contingency detail lives) → required
supplementary information → statistical section (10-year trend tables).

- The **statistical section** is usually the fastest path to multi-year
  trend data (revenue by source, expenditures by function, debt capacity)
  — check there before manually reconstructing trends page by page.
- The **notes** section is where one-time items, changes in accounting
  method, and pending litigation that affects how to interpret a number
  are disclosed. A summary that only uses the statement pages and skips
  the notes will miss the context that changes what a number means.
- Adopted vs. proposed vs. actual budgets are often separate documents
  entirely (proposed budget in spring, adopted in summer, CAFR/actuals
  the following year) — confirm which one you were handed before
  comparing it to anything else.

## Nonprofit tax documents (Form 990 / 990-EZ / 990-PF)

Key parts: Part I (summary — total revenue, expenses, net assets), Part
III (program service accomplishments — narrative), Part VII (officer/
director/key-employee compensation), Part VIII (statement of revenue,
by category), Part IX (functional expenses — program vs. management vs.
fundraising), Schedule A (public support test, for 501(c)(3)s), Schedule
J (executive compensation detail), Schedule O (supplemental narrative —
often where the real explanation of an unusual line item is).

- **Program vs. management vs. fundraising expense ratios** (Part IX)
  are commonly cited but easy to compute wrong — pull the three category
  totals directly rather than re-deriving them from sub-lines.
- Compensation reported on the 990 (Part VII) is for the calendar year
  ending within the organization's fiscal year, which may not equal the
  amount in the fiscal-year financial statements — don't conflate the two
  sources.
- 990s are filed with a lag (often 12+ months after fiscal year end) —
  always record which fiscal year the 990 covers, not the filing date.

## General financial statements (revenue/expense statements, grant reports)

- Check whether figures are **budget-to-actual** (two columns, a
  variance) or single-column actuals only — a summary that treats a
  budget figure as an actual (or vice versa) misrepresents performance.
- Grant reports often mix **award amount**, **amount drawn down/spent
  to date**, and **remaining balance** — these are three different
  numbers commonly confused with each other.
- Look for a **reporting period** on every table (fiscal year, quarter,
  cumulative-to-date) — the same-looking number can mean very different
  things depending on the period it covers.

## Cross-document comparisons (multi-year, multi-entity)

When asked to compare across documents (e.g. three years of a city
budget, or a peer set of similar-size cities' CAFRs):

1. Extract each document independently first, preserving its own labels
   and fund structure — don't force early alignment.
2. Build a crosswalk of line-item labels across documents (the same
   fund or category is often labeled slightly differently year to year
   or city to city).
3. Flag any year/entity where a category doesn't have a clean match
   rather than forcing an approximate one silently — note the
   discrepancy in the output instead.
4. Normalize units (thousands vs. millions vs. actual) before comparing
   — this is the most common source of a wildly wrong comparison chart.
